Think about a student who writes their essay from memory, then flicks through a textbook to find quotes that agree with what they already wrote. The quotes are real. They even support the essay. But they weren’t the reason the essay says what it says. AI does the same thing. The two rows on the left show the difference. Faithful: the model reads the source first and the answer follows from it. Post-rationalised: the model decides on an answer from what it already knows, then attaches a citation that happens to agree. Both look identical on the page: an answer with a correct citation. It matters because a citation is supposed to tell you where an answer came from. If the answer would have been the same without the source, the citation tells you nothing about whether to trust it. The diagram on the right is our fix in one picture: the input and our two checks, Alibi and Jenga, work back and forth against each other, and only then does an output come out.

Here’s the journey of one question, following the path. Top row, left to right. It starts with your PDFs and a question, for example “is this candidate a good fit for Palantir?” The citation search agent, Claude Sonnet 5.5, investigates using five tools: list files, PDF info, search, read pages and select evidence. Every line of PDF text gets an address like “page 1, line 11”, and the agent has to cite those addresses. The key design decision is the full log. We record everything: what the agent wrote before each tool call, and the exact text each tool showed it. That log is our evidence. Then the path turns, and our two checks run on that log. First the Alibi check, in blue: was the evidence there? Then the Jenga check, in amber: did the answer actually use it? The output is the answer you asked for, with a verdict on every claim in it.

Alibi answers one narrow question: did the agent pull the facts out of the PDF correctly? A judge model, Claude Haiku 5.5, breaks every claim into atomic facts, one checkable statement each. For each fact it says whether a line in the PDF states it, and which line. Then code, not the model, does the counting. On our test answer, 12 out of 12 facts were found in the PDF. This is a direct count over every fact in the answer, not a sample, so we report the number itself with no confidence interval. Each claim then gets one of four verdicts, and the rule is simple: a claim’s verdict is its worst fact. Supported: every fact is in the lines it cites. Miscited: every fact is in the PDF, but not all in the lines the claim pointed to. Not found: at least one fact isn’t in the PDF at all. Contradicted: the PDF says something different. The claim’s kind, a document fact, a judgement or outside knowledge, is shown beside it but doesn’t change the rule. For an opinion, we check the facts it rests on. If asked for a source: the atomic-fact approach follows FActScore (Min et al., 2023).

Jenga has two layers. Layer one is free: it reads the agent’s log and scores the answer’s traceability from 0 to 100, with no extra model calls. It rates the AI’s answer, not the document: a weak CV described accurately still scores high. Our test answer scored 74. Each claim is a tower of its cited lines. Load-bearing: the only source of one of the claim’s terms. Supporting: helps, but another line covers it too. Dead weight: removing it loses nothing. Phantom: the agent never even saw that line. The formula: every claim starts at 100 and loses points, d, for each problem, and can’t go below zero. The answer’s traceability is the average over its claims. The penalties, as on the slide. 50 for no citation: the claim can’t be traced at all. 50 for a phantom line: cited but never shown to the agent. 40 for “said before seen”: the agent wrote a value before any tool result contained it. 25 for bridging: the question’s focus is in the claim but not in the cited lines. Up to 20 for dead weight, scaled by the share of cited lines that carry nothing. There are also smaller ones: 10 per term that appears in no source, up to 30; 5 per term found only outside the citation, up to 15; and 5 for citing lines the agent never marked as evidence. We’re upfront that these weights are hand-set and the checks compare words. So this layer shows whether a citation can carry its claim. Layer two, next, tests whether the answer actually depended on it.

Built on the ERASER framework

Layer two is the real experiment. We change what the agent can read, rerun the same agent with the same question, and count how often the claim comes back. Three tests. Test one, the measurement: cited lines taken out. We blank the lines the claim cites and rerun 35 to 40 times, until the 95% interval on the result is at most 10 points wide. If the claim comes back in under 20% of runs, it needed those lines: faithful. Over 50%, it stands without its evidence: post-rationalised. In between, partly. If it comes back by citing other lines that say the same thing, that is redundant evidence, not post-rationalisation. Test two: a value changed. We swap a value in the PDF for a plausible wrong one, for example 4 projects becomes 6. If the answer now says 6, it is reading the document. If it still says 4, it is ignoring the document and answering from its own belief. Test three: all PDF text taken out. We blank every line and ask again. If the agent still makes the claim, it can produce it from memory, so the citation may be decoration. Tests two and three run 5 times each, so they only catch strong effects. There is also a control, nothing changed, run 5 times: if the agent doesn't make the claim reliably even with the PDF untouched, the other tests can't be read and the claim is marked unstable. Details if asked. The reruns use the same model that wrote the answer, Claude Sonnet 5.5, because a different model would measure a different system. Claims are matched across reruns by their anchor terms: the names, numbers and tools in the cited lines. The idea of removing evidence and measuring the change comes from the ERASER framework.

UPPER BOUND WHEN 0 OF n SURVIVE

SOLVE FOR n

z = 1.96 for a 95% interval

How many reruns are enough? We didn’t pick 35 out of the air. Say every rerun agrees: the claim never survives. With zero survivals out of n runs, the upper end of the 95% Wilson interval has a simple closed form: z squared over n plus z squared, where z is 1.96. That’s the highest survival rate still consistent with what we saw. We want that below 10%. Solve for n and you get z squared times nine, which is 34.6, so 35 runs. The chart shows why fewer isn’t enough. After 5 agreeing runs, the true survival rate could still be as high as 43%. Put another way: a claim that truly survives 40% of the time still shows zero out of five about 8% of the time, so five runs can’t rule it out. The curve crosses the 20% line, our bar for calling a claim faithful, at around 15 runs, and reaches the 10% target at 35. The interval narrows like one over the square root of n, so halving it takes four times the runs. And if the runs disagree, say 20 out of 40, the interval stays wide, 35 to 65%, so we stop at 40 and label from the interval we have.

Here’s one real claim taken all the way through, from a real CV, anonymised. The free static check had flagged this claim, “as Director of Projects you oversee 4 projects”, as a post-rationalisation risk. So we measured it properly. We blanked its cited lines and reran the agent 35 times. The claim survived in zero of them: a 95% interval of 0 to 10%. Without its evidence, the claim falls. And when we planted a wrong value, changing 4 projects to 6, the answer said 6 in 4 of 5 runs and dropped the claim in the other: it reads the document. With no PDF text at all it never made the claim, so it isn’t coming from the model’s memory. Now the table on the right. Every claim lands in one of four cells: did Alibi find its facts, and did Jenga show the answer used its evidence? This claim’s facts were found, and it used them, so it lands top left: faithful, the cell with the outline. The other cells. Top right, in amber, is the one we hunt for: cited but not used. The facts are really in the source, so a normal checker passes it, but the answer didn’t depend on them. Bottom left, in red, miscited: the answer leaned on the document but the citation doesn’t back the claim. Bottom right, in grey, unsupported and unused. The measured test cleared a claim the cheap check had flagged, which is exactly why we have both layers. Costs, if asked: answering a question is about 2 cents and 25 seconds. The Alibi judge is a tenth of a cent. The static score is free. Measuring one claim with 40 reruns is about 62 cents and 72 seconds, so it’s opt-in per claim and cached afterwards.

Three directions we want to take this. Research. We want to write a paper on post-rationalisation: models, or people, writing a claim first and finding a source to support it afterwards. Every run of our system produces a detailed log of what the AI saw and when it said what, so the logs themselves become a dataset for studying it. New domains. Anywhere a citation carries weight: legal briefs, policy reports, journalism and fact-checking. In those places a citation that wasn’t really used can do real damage. Integrations. Rather than a separate website, the checks could sit inside the tools people already use: reference managers, journal submission systems, or an AI model’s own citation step, so a claim gets checked before anything is published.

Built for the Read the Machine track at the OxAI Making Sense hackathon

To wrap up. AI tools now cite sources for everything, and we instinctively trust an answer more when it has a source. Jalibi checks whether that trust is earned: Alibi asks whether the evidence was there, Jenga asks whether the AI actually used it. It works today: upload PDFs, ask a question, and see a cited answer with a verdict for every claim, and every step the AI took. We built this for the Read the Machine track here at the OxAI Making Sense hackathon, which is about showing what an AI agent actually did. That is exactly what Jalibi does for citations. Limits, if asked: the free checks compare words, so they show a citation could support a claim, not that the model relied on it. Only rerunning the agent proves that. And we’ve tested on a small set of documents so far. Thank you. Happy to take questions, or show it live.
